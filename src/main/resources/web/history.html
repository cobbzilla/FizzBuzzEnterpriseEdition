Company History

We were asked to count to one hundred. We built an institution capable of explaining why this required an institution. Progress is measured in approval chains per integer, a metric on which we have become almost impossible to outperform.

Confounding the courage to convene

The Serious Business Company, Inc. began with a straightforward commercial insight: a loop could sustain far more careers if nobody was allowed to write it directly. Our founders saw a reporting structure waiting to happen and bravely declined to ask whether the numbers had requested one.

Our earliest investments established a voting franchise for loop continuation, certified succession for every integer, and a notary empowered to ask whether “FizzBuzz” really meant “FizzBuzz.” The answer remained reassuringly familiar. The number of people metaphorically responsible for obtaining it became a significant growth story.

We subsequently required One to complete recruitment and probation before counting could begin. This solved the long-neglected problem of a starting constant arriving without an employee handbook. Our confidence in the necessity of this intervention remains well ahead of any evidence that anyone else needed it.

A legacy of disciplined expansion

Transactions acquired an audit trail. The audit trail acquired a database, a message queue, and offices prepared to inspect the offices inspecting it. Operational rehearsals, an API charter, and a public showcase followed. Eventually the entire Java enterprise moved into the browser, allowing us to bring an unreasonable amount of organization directly to the person who had only wanted to see fifteen.

Procurement now accounts for each individual letter. Repeated z’s carry separate receiving papers. Reusing an answer requires a court and a Certificate of Authorized Non-Performance, because even doing no new work should provide meaningful employment for our abstractions.

Today our mission remains unchanged: deliver the correct answer while ensuring that no contributing abstraction feels underappreciated. The answer has shown very little interest in our organizational maturity. This is a fictional company and a software parody; the immense confidence of our documentation remains an internal achievement rather than an external certification.

The corporate archives

Two complementary records preserve our escalating commitment to this exercise. One explains why every additional office is a triumph. The other records the emotional consequences of believing it. Both grow with each improvement; the game rules have so far resisted comparable expansion.
